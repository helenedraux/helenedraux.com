# Interviewing yourself


I made a writing tone document for AI by interviewing myself, which sounds more indulgent than it felt. The practical problem was simple: I needed a model to write closer to my actual voice, and describing my preferences directly was not enough. Stated preferences are too tidy. They miss the places where practice behaves differently from self-description, and those are usually the places that matter.

I gave the model a writing archive first and asked it not to draft anything until it had conducted a calibration conversation. The important part was that the model had to compare what I said about my writing with what the archive showed, then surface contradictions rather than smoothing them away. I did not want a style guide built from my self-image as a writer; I wanted one built from the gap between what I thought I did and what I had actually done on the page.

The interview had three parts. First, the model asked focused questions about how I know a piece is finished, which posts had failed, and what I expect the reader to do with the argument. Then it ran a sentence-level taste test: five short passages interpreting the same invented bibliometric finding in different registers, which I had to rank and explain. Finally, it named contradictions between my stated preferences and the archive, then asked me to resolve them.

The taste test did most of the useful work. I could say that I disliked “punchy” writing, but that was too vague to help a model. When I ranked the passages, the distinction became more precise: I did not object to short sentences as such, I objected when they closed down the finding before the complexity had been carried. That is a much better instruction than “do not be punchy,” which mostly invites the model to become solemn in a blazer.

The process also surfaced something I had not articulated: in many of my posts, the finding is the vehicle for the method rather than the other way round. I am often less interested in the empirical result as a destination than in what the result lets me show about a method, a proxy, a classification, or an analytical limit. I recognised that immediately once the model named it, but I would not have written it unprompted.

The other useful correction was about dry wit. I would have said I used it rarely. The archive suggested otherwise, or at least suggested a more specific rule. The wit appears when it is directed at myself, the process, or the material; it does not work when it points at the reader, and it becomes unbearable when it tries to signal that the writer is cleverer than the thing being described. That distinction now sits in the tone document because the interview forced it into view.

I do not think this works without an archive. A model needs enough writing to observe patterns rather than simply accept claims about them, and the prompt has to make the calibration conversation part of the method rather than a polite prelude before drafting. The guide at the end is useful, but the conversation is where the useful self-knowledge appears, which is mildly inconvenient for anyone hoping to skip straight to the template.
