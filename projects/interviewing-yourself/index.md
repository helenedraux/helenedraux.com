# Interviewing yourself

The standard way to produce a writing style guide for an AI is to describe your preferences and hand them over. The problem is that stated preferences and actual practice diverge in ways the writer cannot always see: you say you avoid hedging, but hedge in specific contexts; you say you use dry wit sparingly, but the archive shows it appearing in a consistent pattern you had not consciously named.

The approach here was different. The prompt asked the model to read the full archive first, then conduct a calibration conversation before writing anything — and to surface contradictions between what I said and what the posts showed, rather than flatten them into the guide.

The calibration had three moves. First, focused questions about what "finished" feels like from the inside, which post failed and why, and what the ideal reader does with the piece. Second, a sentence-level taste test: five short passages interpreting the same invented bibliometric finding in different registers, each genuinely different, with a request to rank them and explain the preference. Third, explicit contradiction-surfacing: where stated preference conflicted with observed practice, the model named the tension and asked me to resolve it.

What the conversation surfaced that I would not have written unprompted: the inversion at the centre of how I structure posts. Most data journalism uses methodology as an excuse to get to the finding. I use the finding as an excuse to present the methodology. The empirical result is evidence for a methodological claim, not the point in itself. The model named this precisely — "the finding is the vehicle" — after I described two draft posts and it recognised the pattern across both. I had not articulated it in those terms before.

The taste test was the most useful single step. Ranking five passages forced a sentence-level preference that questions about style cannot produce: I could say I disliked "punchy" writing, but the model noticed that I liked one passage that used short sentences and disliked another that did the same thing, and asked what the difference was. The answer — that B and C closed down the finding while D and E held the complexity open — was more precise than "punchy" and more actionable for the guide.

The contradiction the model surfaced: dry wit appears in the archive more than I would have said. Resolving it clarified something: the wit is always self-directed or subject-directed, never reader-directed, never used to signal that the writer is smarter than the material. That distinction is not one I would have volunteered, but it is one I would recognise immediately as accurate.

Whether this process is replicable depends on the archive. It requires enough published writing for the model to observe patterns rather than just accept assertions — and it requires a prompt that explicitly instructs the model to run the calibration before producing anything, rather than treating the conversation as optional scaffolding. The prompt is the method; the conversation is the output that matters, not just the guide at the end.
